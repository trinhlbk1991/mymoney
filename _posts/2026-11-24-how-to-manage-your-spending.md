Pay arrives on the 5th. By the 20th your balance looks thin, and you can't say where it went. If that sounds familiar, you're probably not careless. Spending today is scattered: a QR scan here, a delivery order there, an automatic charge that renews every month. Each one is small, and none of them feels like "spending money".

This guide is a five-step system plus a monthly review. Do the steps one at a time, not all at once. Later posts go deeper on each step and link back here.

## Step 1: See where your money goes for 30 days

The first job isn't cutting back. It's **looking**. For 30 days, write down every payment: food, transport, rent, coffee, subscriptions, even the cash you hand over at the market.

A few tips so this month doesn't get abandoned:

- **Record it the same day.** Wait until the weekend and you'll forget half of it.
- **Keep categories few.** Five to eight is plenty: housing, food, transport, bills, shopping, fun, health, other.
- **Don't judge yet.** This month is for collecting data. If you track and scold yourself at the same time, you'll stop tracking.
- **Check your statements.** Bank statements and e-wallet history help you catch what you forgot.

After 30 days you'll have two things: your real monthly total and your three biggest categories. Almost everyone finds at least one number that surprises them. That's why this step is worth doing.

## Step 2: Split your income: savings first, fixed bills, then the rest to spend

Once you know where the money goes, split your take-home pay into three parts, in this order:

1. **Save first.** Move it as soon as pay arrives, to a separate account or wallet. What's "left over at month-end" is usually nothing.
2. **Fixed costs.** The things that barely change: rent, utilities, internet, phone, instalments.
3. **What remains is your spending budget.** This is money you're free to spend on food, transport, shopping and fun.

The order matters. If you spend first and save what's left, you'll save very little. Flip it, and saving becomes a bill that's already paid, not something that takes willpower every month.

### Example: take-home pay of 15,000,000₫

These figures are only an example. Swap in your own.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Take-home pay | 15,000,000₫ |
| Savings | 2,000,000₫ |
| Rent | 3,500,000₫ |
| Utilities and internet | 800,000₫ |
| Phone | 200,000₫ |
| Instalments | 500,000₫ |
| **Total fixed costs** | **5,000,000₫** |
| **Spending budget** | **8,000,000₫** |

The sums: 3,500,000 + 800,000 + 200,000 + 500,000 = 5,000,000₫ in fixed costs. Savings plus fixed costs is 2,000,000 + 5,000,000 = 7,000,000₫. Take that from 15,000,000 and **8,000,000₫** is left to spend over the month.

The numbers are in đồng, but the method works in any currency. Only the figures change.

Here savings are 2,000,000₫, about 13% of pay. If you can't manage that in a given month, start smaller. Regular beats big.

## Step 3: Set a monthly budget and limits for 2–3 categories that run over

The 8,000,000₫ above is your **monthly budget**. You could stop here, since one overall budget beats none. For a bit more control, add separate limits for **two or three categories you tend to overspend**, the ones that ballooned in Step 1.

For many people that's eating out, delivery, online shopping or coffee. Don't set limits for everything. Too many numbers wears you out, and you quit.

Some rules of thumb:

- **Base limits on the real numbers** from your first month, not what you *wish* you spent.
- **Keep them realistic.** A budget that's too tight breaks in week one, and you'll feel like you failed when the numbers were the problem.
- **Leave room for the unexpected.** Something always comes up: a gift, a repair, a doctor's visit.

## Step 4: Spend by a daily number

A budget of 8,000,000₫ for the whole month is hard to feel. Against it, a 200,000₫ dinner doesn't seem like much. So break it down.

**8,000,000₫ ÷ 30 days ≈ 266,000₫ a day** (rounded down).

Now you have an easy question to ask each day: *how much can I spend today?* Spend less and tomorrow's number rises. Spend more and it dips. You see the effect of each choice right away, while you can still adjust, instead of getting a shock at month-end.

The formula, and how to recalculate midway through a month, are in the [safe-to-spend method guide](/guides/safe-to-spend-budget/). The idea: the daily number is what's left of your budget divided by the days left, so you recalculate after every payment.

## Step 5: Make tracking automatic so you don't quit

This is the step that decides whether the system survives week two. People rarely stop tracking because they're lazy. They stop because each payment means opening an app, choosing a category and typing an amount, dozens of times a week.

If you pay by QR and e-wallet, almost every payment sends a notification to your phone. That notification already holds the amount, who you paid and when: everything you were about to type. Using it is the lightest way to avoid retyping.

That's what myMoney's [auto-capture](/auto-capture/) does: it reads the payment notification from your bank or e-wallet and creates the transaction, without logging in to your bank. You only review it and fix it when needed.

Whatever tool you use, the principle holds: **the fewer manual steps, the longer the system lasts.** Notebooks and spreadsheets work fine if you really keep them up. Just ask yourself honestly: in week three, will you still be typing?

## At month-end: a 15-minute review

At the end of each month, take 15 minutes, no more, and answer four questions:

1. **Did total spending stay within budget?** If not, by how much?
2. **Which category ran over, and why?** A habit, or a one-off?
3. **Did you save what you planned?** If not, what could move?
4. **What's one change for next month?** One, not ten.

Don't rebuild the system every month. After two or three months your budget will fit real life much better, and the review becomes routine.

## Common mistakes

**A budget that's too tight.** Cutting out coffee, outings and eating out to get a neat number on paper often leads to one big blow-out week, then giving up. A budget with room to breathe lasts longer.

**Forgetting cash.** Cash payments (the market, small stalls, lì xì, wedding gifts) leave no notification, so they slip through. Write them down as you pay, or at day's end.

**No emergency cushion.** When your bike breaks or you get sick, with no buffer all of it lands in your monthly budget and wrecks it. Part of the savings in Step 2 should be for this.

**Starting too much at once.** You don't need a complex spreadsheet, ten categories and three wallets in month one. Do Step 1, settle in, then add.

**Quitting after one broken month.** A month over budget isn't failure, it's data. See where it went over, change one thing, carry on.

## In short

Managing spending isn't about living tight. It's knowing where your money goes, deciding in advance what to save and what to spend, and having one simple number each day to lean on. Start with 30 days of tracking, because every later step needs real numbers.
